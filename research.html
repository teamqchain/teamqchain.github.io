Research

Why lattice signatures, and what they cost a blockchain.

The algorithms ↓ The architecture ↓
Threat
Shor’s algorithm breaks ECDSA and RSA. On Fabric that means forged identities and forged transactions.
Standard
ML-DSA (Dilithium) for signatures, ML-KEM (Kyber) for encryption. NIST FIPS 204 and 203.
Trade-off
An ML-DSA-44 signature is 2,420 bytes, about 38 times an ECDSA one.
Design
Keep only salted hash commitments on-chain. Put the encrypted credential on IPFS.

The threat

The ledger is permanent. The cryptography under it isn’t.

Anything recorded today can be attacked later. Hashes survive a quantum computer; the signatures that prove who wrote them do not.

ECDSA · RSA-2048

Broken

Shor’s algorithm solves them in polynomial time. MSP admin certificates and transaction signatures can be forged.

SHA-256 · SHA3-256

Holds at 256 bits

Grover’s algorithm halves the effective strength. A 256-bit hash still leaves 128 bits.

ML-DSA · ML-KEM

Post-quantum

Built on lattice problems with no known efficient quantum attack. Standardised as FIPS 204 and 203.

The algorithms

Three candidates. One fits a ledger.

Validators verify far more often than anyone signs, and every byte of signature is stored forever. That decides it.

ML-DSA-44 DilithiumFIPS 2041,312 B2,420 B~0.5 ms~0.1 ms Bigger than Falcon. Our pick.

The cost

Bigger signatures, slower blocks.

Signature size, to scale

FN-DSA666 B
ML-DSA-442,420 B

Sizes from the NIST PQC specifications.

Fabric throughput

ECDSA secp256r1~3,000 TPS
ML-DSA-44~1,250 TPS

An estimated 50–60% drop without hardware acceleration, from verification time and larger packets. It is the reason credential bodies live on IPFS and only commitments go on-chain.
